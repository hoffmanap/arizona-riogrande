CELL-PHONE MOBILITY DATA — GROUNDING THE RECOMMENDATIONS

Mobility & Visitation Patterns

Arizona-Rio Grande subarea, El Paso -- anonymized device-location data (SafeGraph / Ubermedia-style panel, ~1,100 devices, dwell-time and pathing observations across the subarea) used to test three questions: is commercial activity here serving the neighborhood or pulling from a wider region; which streets actually carry the foot traffic that could support more commercial use; and which streets are already vehicle-dominated enough that limiting them to single-family makes little sense. See the subdistrict story and the existing land-use map for the zoning side of this argument, and the visit density & traffic map for place- and street-level detail behind these findings.

01 / LOCAL VS. REGIONAL DRAW

Who is this corridor actually serving?

For every device that visited the subarea, its home and work locations were matched against distance bands from the corridor. This tells us whether retail and services here are neighborhood-serving or pulling from across the metro.

52% of visits come from within 3 miles -- home-location and work-location bands land within a point of each other, so this isn't commuters passing through on their way somewhere else. The corridor is overwhelmingly a neighborhood-serving draw, which argues for zoning that permits small-format, walkable commercial uses (personal services, food, small retail) rather than planning for regional-draw anchors that need highway visibility and large parking fields.

02 / DWELL TIME

People linger here -- this isn't a drive-through corridor

Dwell time measures how long a device stayed once it arrived, split into the time spent at its most-visited stop ("inner") vs. anywhere else in the subarea ("outer"), across 5,247 measured visits.

Median dwell time is 25 minutes, and 32% of visits run an hour or longer (hairdressers, legal/medical appointments, restaurants, the furniture store). A corridor where a third of visits last an hour-plus is doing destination business, not just capturing pass-by traffic -- another argument for protecting and expanding small-format commercial rather than treating these blocks as a pure drive corridor.

03 / STREET-LEVEL MODE SPLIT

Which streets are already walked, and which are already driven

Every device movement inside the subarea was classified as pedestrian or vehicular by speed, then assigned to the nearest street. This tells us where foot traffic already exists (so commercial uses there have a built-in customer base) and where traffic is already heavily vehicular (so planning those blocks around single-family makes little sense regardless of zoning history).

Missouri is both the single busiest street in the subarea and the most vehicular -- 23,655 measured segments, 64% of them vehicular. Yandell, Lee, Newman, and Octavia are majority-pedestrian and well-suited to walkable, small-format retail. Missouri's volume alone makes it a poor candidate for single-family-only zoning: whatever the historic zoning says, the ground truth is a busy, car-dominated corridor that should permit (and may already have) commercial uses with real transportation planning, not a residential street that happens to carry 15,000+ vehicle movements.

04 / WHEN STREETS ARE BUSY

Missouri's pattern is commuter-shaped, not weekend-leisure

The same classified movements, totaled by day of week for the five busiest streets. A weekday-peaking pattern reads as commuting and errands; a weekend-peaking pattern reads as leisure and discretionary retail trips -- useful for deciding what kind of commercial mix fits each street.

Missouri peaks midweek (Wed/Thu) and dips hardest on Sunday -- a commuter and errand-running pattern consistent with its vehicular majority above, which favors uses that serve people already moving through (quick service, auto-related, professional offices) over destination leisure retail. Yandell and Wyoming hold steadier totals across the whole week, including weekends -- more consistent with neighborhood-serving, walk-up commercial.

05 / PLACE-LEVEL VISIT DENSITY

Which buildings are working hardest for their square footage

Each device ping was assigned to the specific SafeGraph place polygon it fell inside, then counted as a unique visit (one device, one day). Visits per square foot of building area shows which uses are generating the most activity relative to their footprint -- the uses worth protecting and replicating as the corridor is rezoned.

By raw volume, the corridor's two busiest addresses are 1025/1021 E Missouri Ave (American Furniture Outlet & Glamour Cult Salon, sharing one building -- 5,855 unique visits, nearly 10/day). By density (visits per square foot), small professional-service and live/work buildings outperform everything else -- legal services and physicians' offices alone account for over 60,000 of the subarea's measured unique visits across just 132 places, more than any other use category, while occupying a comparatively small footprint. That's the strongest evidence in this dataset for protecting and expanding small-office and personal-service uses alongside retail, not just planning around a handful of larger commercial anchors.

06 / WHAT THIS MEANS FOR THE REZONING

Three findings, three zoning implications

Scale the use mix to a local draw

52% local, <3% pure commuters passing through. Plan for neighborhood-serving small retail, personal services, and food — not regional anchors needing highway visibility or large parking fields.

Match commercial allowances to where feet already are

Yandell, Lee, Newman, and Octavia are majority-pedestrian today. Commercial-use allowances on these streets build on an existing pedestrian base rather than creating one from scratch.

Don't zone a vehicular corridor as single-family

Missouri carries more measured traffic than any other street in the subarea and is majority-vehicular with a commuter-shaped weekly pattern. Whatever the historic zoning says, the ground truth argues for uses built around that traffic, not single-family lots facing a de facto arterial.
