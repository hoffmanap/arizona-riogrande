03 / DOES TRAFFIC MEAN BUSINESS?

High-traffic streets and high-visit businesses only partly overlap

Every street's total measured movement (foot + vehicle) plotted against the total unique visits its storefronts actually received, by matching each place's street address to the 21 subarea streets (398 of 398 places matched). If traffic volume reliably predicted business visits, these points would line up on a clean diagonal -- they don't.

What the correlation says r = 0.66 across 17 streets with any storefronts

A moderate, not a strong, relationship. Traffic volume explains some but far from all of the variation in how much business activity a street supports -- some streets clearly carry pass-through volume with little to show for it in visits, and others support more business than their traffic volume alone would predict.

Heavy traffic, light visits (pass-through)Ange, Noble, Lee
Modest traffic, high visits/place (destination)Nevada, Missouri

Reading the outliers

Ange carries the 6th-most total movement in the subarea but has only one storefront and 218 visits -- almost purely a through-street. Noble and Lee show the same pattern at a smaller scale. By contrast, Missouri and Montana both convert heavy traffic into heavy visits (they anchor the top-right of the chart), while Nevada -- middling traffic, only two storefronts -- still produces the highest average visits per place in the subarea (915), suggesting a couple of genuine destinations rather than pass-by volume. The zoning implication: traffic counts alone can't tell you which streets are already functioning as commercial corridors versus which just happen to carry cars or pedestrians through a residential block.
